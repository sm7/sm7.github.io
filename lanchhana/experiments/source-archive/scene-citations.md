# Scene citations (verbatim from scenes.json)


## ayodhya: Setting · Ayodhyā and Kosala in the Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa

### The city on the Sarayū: wall, moat, gates and gardens [city]
- Attested: Kosala lies on the bank of the Sarayū, and Ayodhyā was built by Manu (Rāmāyaṇa 1.5.5–6). The city is twelve yojanas long and three wide, with well-planned highways (1.5.7). It has gateways and archways, a deep, hard-to-cross moat and a great wall like a girdle, with mango groves and gardens around it, and it holds horses, elephants, cows, camels and donkeys (1.5.10–13). Its plan is like an aṣṭāpada game-board, on level ground, built so densely that no plot is left unused (1.5.16–17).
- Reading note: The translation used renders 'sāla-mekhalā' as a fort-wall, where Griffith has a belt of sāl trees; the painting follows the wall. The size, twelve by three yojanas, is many miles and is not drawn to scale; only the long, narrow shape is kept.
- Conjecture: The verses give no building material for the wall, so its brick, crenellations and corner towers are the painter's. The number and form of the gates, the bridges, the sailing boats on the river and the orange pennants are not in the text. The roofs and the form of the larger palaces are not described either. Colour: the text gives white only for the houses of the royal road and the tall palace buildings (2.17.2, 2.17.18) and, as Bharata's comparison, for the city seen from afar (2.71.19); the painter made nearly all the houses white.

### A city gate: tribute, trade and craftsmen [gate]
- Attested: The city is ringed with gateways and archways and has well-planned shop-fronts inside (1.5.10). It holds craftsmen and all kinds of machines and weapons (1.5.10), crowds of bards and eulogists, bastions with flags (1.5.11), troupes of dancers and actors (1.5.12), provincial kings bringing dues and traders from many countries (1.5.14), and the sound of great drums, mṛdaṅgas and vīṇās (1.5.18). Thousands of skilled archers and chariot-warriors live there (1.5.20–22).
- Reading note: Verse 1.5.11 also names the śataghnī, which the translation calls a 'cannon'. It is a weapon of uncertain form and is not painted.
- Conjecture: The carved stone gate-towers with relief elephants, the wooden pavilions on top, the sun-emblem flags, the forge and sword stalls, the pack-camels and the dancers' stitched costumes are the painter's. The text does not say what the gate was built of or how it looked.

### The royal road, watered and strewn with flowers [road]
- Attested: The great royal road is straight, well laid out and always sprinkled with water, with scattered flowers (1.5.8). It is clean, perfumed with aloe incense and lined with white houses like clouds (2.17.1–2). It holds heaps of sandal, aloe-wood, silk and linen cloth, unpierced pearls, crystal, flowers and eatables (2.17.3–5). At every crossroads offerings of curd, unbroken rice, ghee, parched grain, incense and garlands are laid (2.17.6). Elephants, horses and chariots move along it, and people gather at the junctions (2.16.47).
- Reading note: The verses say the offerings are laid at all crossroads; the painting shows one.
- Conjecture: The paving stone, the cloth awnings, the balconies with creepers, the carved pillar and the carts are not in the verses. The houses are white as the text says, but their storeys, balconies and ornament are the painter's. Dress and jewellery are conjecture. Colour: the verses call the houses along this road pale-white like clouds (pāṇḍura, 2.17.2); that is the only colour given.

### The city decked for the coronation, at dawn [festival]
- Attested: Before sunrise the citizens decorate the town (2.6.10). Tall banners and coloured flags are raised on temples that look like the silver lining of a cloud, at crossroads, in streets, on the big trees of temple compounds, on watchtowers of the city gates, on merchants' shops, on the houses of the rich, on all assembly halls and on conspicuous trees (2.6.11–13). Actors, dancers and singers perform on all sides (2.6.14). Children play at house gateways and talk of the coronation (2.6.15–16). The royal road is strewn with flowers and perfumed with incense (2.6.17).
- Reading note: The comparison of temple tops with 'the lining of a cloud' gives a colour and a height, not a form.
- Conjecture: The conical white temple spires, the pointed gate arch, the balconies, the dancers' stitched skirts and blouses and the colours of the banners are the painter's. The text gives no shape for the temples. Colour: only the temple tops are given a colour, like the lining of a white cloud (2.6.11); the white houses and pale paving are the painter's.

### Night: lamps in the shape of trees [night]
- Attested: Fearing night would fall before the day's rites ended, the citizens set up lamps in the shape of trees to light all the streets (2.6.18). The road is strewn with flowers and incense (2.6.17). Villagers from every direction fill the city to see the ceremony (2.6.25–26), and the noise is like the roaring sea on a full-moon night (2.6.27–28).
- Second source: No second source is used for this scene.
- Conjecture: The golden branching lamp-stands, the royal chariot and elephants in the street, the sun-emblem banners and the flat-roofed terraces are the painter's. The verse says only 'lamps in the shape of trees'. The full moon is taken from the simile. Colour: the white terraces are the painter's; the verses give no colour for the houses.

### The coronation things laid out [prep]
- Attested: Brahmans skilled in the Vedas wait with the priest Vasiṣṭha; ministers, army chiefs and leading citizens gather (2.15.1–2). The things prepared are golden pots, a decorated throne, a chariot covered with tiger skin (2.15.4), water from the confluence of the Gaṅgā and Yamunā and from many rivers and seas (2.15.5–6), gold and silver pots with parched grain, leaves, lotuses and water-lilies (2.15.7), honey, curd, ghee, parched grain, kuśa grass, flowers and milk (2.15.8), a golden fly-whisk and a white umbrella like the moon (2.15.9–10), a white bull, a white horse and a mighty elephant (2.15.11), and musicians and bards (2.15.12). Kings from many countries wait with the crowd (2.15.13–16).
- Left out: The same verses (2.15.8, 2.15.12) name courtesans and eight auspicious maidens; the image filter refused them, so they are not shown.
- Conjecture: The placing of the objects on a plaza by a river, the rugs, the form of the throne, the dress of the priests and the setting sun are the painter's. The text says only 'a nearby place'.

### Rāma's palace and the crowd at its gate [palace]
- Attested: Sumantra sees Rāma's palace, shining like a mountain peak and like Indra's palace (2.15.31). It has large doors and hundreds of raised seats, festoons of gems and coral over the doorways, pearls scattered about, the scent of sandal and aloe, cranes and peacocks calling, figures of wolves and other forms sculpted on it, and it is full of birds and deer, 'high as Meru' (2.15.32–45). Country people wait outside with gifts (2.15.46), and the elephant Śatruñjaya stands there (2.15.47). Hunchbacked servants and Kirāta attendants move about (2.15.32–40).
- Second source: No second source is used for this scene.
- Conjecture: The stepped, terraced mass of the palace, the white stone, the carved timber gate and the arrangement of courtyards are the painter's. The text gives height and ornament, not plan. The howdah on the elephant is not mentioned. Colour: the verses give no colour for Rāma's palace, only that it shines and is high; its white stone is the painter's.

### Rāma rides to his father's palace [procession]
- Attested: Rāma mounts a chariot covered with tiger skin, decorated with gold and jewels (2.16.27–30). Lakṣmaṇa stands behind, holding an umbrella and a chāmara over him (2.16.32). Horsemen and soldiers on elephants follow (2.16.34); armoured men with swords and bows walk ahead announcing him (2.16.35). Musicians, bards and the roars of warriors are heard (2.16.36). Women in the upper porticoes and turrets of the palaces scatter flowers on him (2.16.37–42). On the way he sees the clean royal road with elephants, horses and chariots (2.16.47).
- Reading note: The verses call Rāma 'dark' in complexion elsewhere (2.2.53, 'like a black lotus flower'); they never say blue.
- Conjecture: Rāma's blue skin is a later iconographic convention, not in the verse. The brass horns, the harness and the wheel type, the design of the balconies, and the crowd's clothing are the painter's. The chariot's horses are shown in white; the verse gives no colour.

### Daśaratha's palace: guards and enclosures [precinct]
- Attested: Rāma crosses three enclosures in his chariot, guarded by men with bows, and the other two on foot, sending the crowd back (2.17.20–21). The palace blazes with radiance, with turrets like Mount Kailāsa and like clouds, tall white houses touching the sky, and royal houses called vardhamāna hung with nets of precious stones (2.17.17–19). In the inner quarters young men with darts and bows and polished ear-rings stand watch (2.16.1–2), and aged supervisors in orange clothes with canes stand at the doors (2.16.3).
- Second source: No second source is used for this scene.
- Conjecture: The marble floor, the gilt and jewelled gate frames, the layout of the enclosures and the distant domed roofs are the painter's. The domes are not in the text, which gives turrets and houses; they are an error of the image. Only the number of enclosures (five) is stated. Colour: the towers are called white like heavenly cars (pāṇḍura, 2.17.18); the white-and-gold marble floor and gate frames are the painter's.

### The inner court of the palace: birds, bowers and gold [queens]
- Attested: In Kaikeyī's apartments of the palace peacocks and parrots are kept, and curlews and swans are heard (2.10.12–16). There are bowers wound with creepers, champaka and aśoka trees, painted rooms, altars and fine seats of ivory, silver and gold, trees that give flowers and fruit in all seasons, wells in the middle, music, and short and hunchbacked attendants (2.10.12–16). Food and drink of many kinds is laid out (2.10.16).
- Reading note: The original prompt for this scene was refused by the image filter because it centred on women. It was repainted as a garden court with attendants and the scene is about the setting, not the queen.
- Conjecture: The pool with lotuses, the courtyard's stone paving, the gilt table, the colour and form of the buildings and the particular musicians are the painter's. The wells appear as one pool; the text says wells. Colour: no colour is given for the walls or floors; the pale plaster is the painter's.

### Bharata comes home to a silent city [silent]
- Attested: Bharata reaches Ayodhyā at dawn after seven nights on the road and sees it from afar like a heap of white clay (2.71.17–19). He notes the parks are deserted, the trees along the road have lost their leaves, no one rides in or out, no sound of drums or vīṇās, no fragrance of aloe and sandal (2.71.21–29). He enters by the Vaijayanta gate and sees houses unswept with their doors open, no offerings or incense, temples deserted and without garlands, traders absent, birds clustered on temples and trees, and people weak, sad and tearful (2.71.33–44). Doors and hinges are rusted (2.71.46). Sumantra had found the same: no one greeting him, women at windows and in seven-storied mansions crying (2.59.10–16).
- Reading note: The 'heap of white clay' is Bharata's comparison for the whitewashed look of the city from afar, not a description of the material.
- Conjecture: The pale stone paving, the particular carved door-frames, the cart with two horses, the vertical timber doors and the number of people are the painter's. Rust is not visible in the painting. The gate is shown as massive wooden doors. Colour: the whiteness comes from Bharata's comparison of the city to a heap of white clay (2.71.19); the pale street and walls are the painter's.

### Rāma enters the city [entry]
- Attested: Sumantra yokes the chariot; Rāma mounts it (6.128.19–20, 6.128.27). Bharata takes the reins, Śatrughna holds the parasol, Lakṣmaṇa a fan and Vibhīṣaṇa a white whisk (6.128.28–29). Sugrīva rides the elephant Śatruñjaya and monkeys, in human form and ornaments, ride nine thousand elephants (6.128.31–32). Conches and kettle-drums sound (6.128.33). Musicians walk in front with cymbals and singing; men carry rice mixed with turmeric that shines gold, with cows, and maidens and brahmans with balls of sweets (6.128.37–38). The citizens raise flags on every house (6.128.42).
- Reading note: The painting shows a few elephants, not nine thousand.
- Conjecture: Which of the brothers stands where, the style of the howdahs and the housefronts, and the dress of the women are the painter's. The chariot's gilded wheel and the number of horses are not in the verse.

### Rāma's consecration [coronation]
- Attested: Vasiṣṭha seats Rāma with Sītā on a seat of precious stones (6.128.59). Vasiṣṭha, Vāmadeva, Kaśyapa, Kātyāyana, Suyajña, Gautama and Vijaya consecrate him with clear, fragrant water (6.128.60–61), and maidens, ministers, warriors and merchants sprinkle sap of herbs (6.128.62). The crown that Manu wore, made by Brahmā, is set on him on a throne of precious stones in the council-hall, studded with gold (6.128.64–67). Śatrughna holds the white parasol, Sugrīva a white whisk and Vibhīṣaṇa another (6.128.68–69). Sugrīva, Vibhīṣaṇa and the vānara chiefs are among those present at the consecration (6.128.84–87). The water was brought in jewelled jars from the four oceans and from five hundred rivers (6.128.49–57).
- Second source: No second source is used for this scene.
- Conjecture: Which figure is Hanumān, his kneeling pose and his monkey face are the painter's; the text says the vānaras took human form for the stay in Ayodhyā (6.127.43, 6.128.32) and gives no form for them at the ceremony. Rāma's dark complexion, the gold-pillared hall with its carved lions and the banners are the painter's. The number and posture of the sages, the ornament of the seat and the dress of the attendants are not in the text. The text does not describe the hall except as 'studded with gold'.

### The country around Ayodhyā [kosala]
- Attested: Rāma, asking Bharata whether he keeps the kingdom as it should be, describes a kingdom adorned with temples and with sheds where water is stored in tanks for travellers, with happy men and women, festivals and gatherings, well-tilled land, cattle free from cruelty, fields not fed by rain alone, free of beasts of prey and fear, and dotted with mines (2.100.43–46). Ayodhyā itself has fortified gates, elephants, horses and chariots, thousands of brahmans, warriors and merchants, and palaces of many shapes (2.100.40–42).
- Reading note: This passage is Rāma's advice, a list of questions about what a good kingdom is like, not a description of Kosala at a moment in the story.
- Conjecture: The waterwheel, sailing boats, the quarry cranes, the thatched village houses and the temple pavilion are the painter's. The verses give the features in the list above and nothing of the layout.

### The kingdom in Rāma's reign [ramarajya]
- Attested: In Rāma's reign there were no widows lamenting, no fear from wild animals or disease, no thieves or robbers (6.128.99–100). Trees flower and fruit regularly, free of pests; clouds rain in due time; the wind is pleasant to touch (6.128.104). The four classes do their own work, content and without greed (6.128.105). People talk of nothing but Rāma (6.128.103).
- Reading note: This is the poem's closing praise of an ideal reign. It names no place, building or landscape, so the painting is a setting for the verses and not a record of any scene.
- Conjecture: The landscape, the city in the distance with domes, the teaching group, the farmers and guards and the market goods are the painter's, chosen to show the four classes at work. The text names no buildings. Colour: no colour is given.

## chalukya: Setting · Pulakeśin II's Deccan, c. 610–642 CE

### Vātāpi (Bādāmi), the capital, under its red cliffs [city]
- Attested: The Aihole inscription says the king, having conquered all quarters, entered the city of Vātāpi and rules the earth "like one city" with the sea for its moat (verse 33; Epigraphia Indica vol. VI no. 1, tr. F. Kielhorn, 1902). It describes nothing of the city itself.
- Surviving site: The site survives at Bādāmi: a valley between two red sandstone hills, a fort on the heights, a large tank, and cave temples cut in the cliff (from the surviving site; not read in a source here).
- Conjecture: Nearly everything: the layout, houses, market, palace compound, people and procession. The fort walls are drawn with regular bastions and the cliffs taller and sheerer than the real site; the fortification's date and form are not established here.

### The army on the march: drums, lances and elephants [army]
- Attested: Xuanzang, who visited c. 640, calls the people tall and stern, "grateful to benefactors, relentless to enemies"; they fight with lances, a band of several hundred champions goes to battle, and drums are beaten before them when they march out; elephants number in many hundreds (Si-yu-ki, Book XI, tr. S. Beal, 1884, vol. 2 pp. 255–258). The Aihole inscription (634–35 CE) gives the king's armies elephants, banners, umbrellas and chowries, and has him cross the Kāverī on elephants (Epigraphia Indica vol. VI no. 1, tr. F. Kielhorn, 1902; a court poem, with a praise-poem's exaggeration).
- Other sources: None: no second source read for dress or arms.
- Conjecture: Dress, arms, the elephants' gear, the river, the numbers and the landscape. The wine-drinking champions and the rutting elephants are in the texts but not shown. The lion on the banner is the model's invention; the dynasty's emblem is the boar.

### Aihole: the stone mansion of Jinendra [temple]
- Attested: The Aihole inscription says the poet Ravikīrti caused a "stone mansion of Jinendra" to be built, and the poem is engraved on it (verses 35–37; dated Śaka 556 = 634–35 CE; Epigraphia Indica vol. VI no. 1, tr. F. Kielhorn, 1902). The text does not describe its form.
- Surviving monument: The form follows the surviving temple at Aihole (from the monument; not read in a source here).
- Conjecture: Everything about its shape and setting, the worshippers, the priest, the seated Jina inside, the trees and hills.

### A rock-cut monastery with a stone Buddha [monastery]
- Attested: Xuanzang describes a monastery in a dark valley with a great mountain behind: lofty halls and deep side-aisles cut through the rock face, storey above storey; in the great vihāra a stone Buddha about 70 feet high under a stone canopy of seven stages; on the four walls, painted scenes of the Bodhisattva's former lives (Si-yu-ki, Book XI, tr. S. Beal, 1884, vol. 2 pp. 255–258). Beal identifies it with Ajanta and notes the measurements are exaggerated.
- Other sources: None: no second source read for the hall's form.
- Conjecture: All form and colour, the hall's proportions, the monks and worshippers. The canopy shows about five tiers, not seven, and the murals are denser than anything the text gives.

## chera: Setting · Muziris under the Cheras, c. 60 CE

### The port: Muziris on its river, c. 60 CE [city]
- Attested: Muziris, in the kingdom of Cerobothra, 'abounds in ships' from Arabia and from the Greeks; it lies on a river, 20 stadia up from the shore; the river is full of shoals so ships anchor at the roadstead of Bacare and cargo is brought down; large ships come for pepper and malabathrum; imports of coin, topaz, linens, coral, glass, copper, tin, lead, wine and wheat; exports of pepper, fine pearls, ivory, silk cloth, spikenard, transparent stones, diamonds, sapphires and tortoise-shell; the kings live in the interior (Periplus of the Erythraean Sea §§53–56, tr. Schoff 1912, pp. 44–45).
- Other sources: None: no second source read (the Sangam poems and Pliny not yet read).
- Conjecture: The town's buildings (thatched sheds here), ship and boat forms, dress, the elephant, the crowd. The text describes only the trade, so this is the only scene.

## chola: Setting · Thanjavur under Rājarāja I, c. 1010–1014 CE

### The capital: the moated temple, the palace to its north, the town [city]
- Attested: The temple faces east; from the east a deep moat, filled at the entrance to make a causeway, then a bastioned wall of fortification, then a heavier enclosure wall about 6 m inside it, with the short, stocky outer gopura and, about 100 m further west, the inner gopura. The palace lay north of the temple. In Rājarāja's 29th year two temple streets, each in two rows of about a hundred houses, were built for the temple's women servants. Tanjavur is called 'surrounded by a moat' in the Tiruvisaippa of Karuvur Devar, the king's guru (S. R. Balasubrahmanyam, Middle Chola Temples, 1975, pp. 16–27, 74, 81–82, citing the inscriptions and the Tiruvisaippa).
- Second source: A modern scholar's account of the inscriptions and the surviving temple. No contemporary description of the town as a whole exists in the sources read.
- Conjecture: The palace's form and size; the plan and extent of the town; the wall and moat shown around the whole town (a moat round the city is attested, a town wall is not); house forms and roofs; the number and spacing of tiers on the tower (the source says thirteen); the rice fields and watercourses. Dress is conjecture: no dress source was read. After review the first paintings were repainted to remove forms that look later than 1010 CE (stitched blouses and draped saris, turbans, a modern dance costume, machine-made tiles, tall gateway towers, woolly sheep); the current wrapped garments and chest bands follow Chola sculpture as the painter remembers it, not a cited source.

### A street outside Tanjore: musicians, elephants, shepherds, bazaar [street]
- Attested: Ten streets lay outside Tanjore: those of the musicians (Gandharva-teru), bowmen, those who cook for elephants, the elephant troops, the temple kitchen (Madaippalli-teru), and four named for Chola kings; the weavers' street lay inside the town; three bazaars lay outside it. Shepherds in these streets kept cows, she-buffaloes and ewes and supplied ghee for the temple's lamps (Rajarajesvara temple inscription No. 94, SII vol. II, ed. Hultzsch and Venkayya, 1913; repeated in Balasubrahmanyam 1975, pp. 81–82).
- Second source: Balasubrahmanyam, Middle Chola Temples (1975), citing the same record.
- Conjecture: House forms, layout, paving, dress and the look of the bazaars. The elephant, musicians and bowmen are inferred from the street names. The flag over the gate in the first painting was the model's addition and has been removed; the sheep are still partly woolly in the painting. Dress is conjecture: no dress source was read. After review the first paintings were repainted to remove forms that look later than 1010 CE (stitched blouses and draped saris, turbans, a modern dance costume, machine-made tiles, tall gateway towers, woolly sheep); the current wrapped garments and chest bands follow Chola sculpture as the painter remembers it, not a cited source.

### The Rājarājeśvara temple, newly complete [temple]
- Attested: A stone temple with a pyramidal tower of thirteen storeys, about 63 m high, topped by a single granite block with Nandis at its corners, a stone cupola and a gilded finial given in the king's 25th year; a cloistered courtyard; two gateways. The inscriptions on its walls record gilt-copper pots, silver dishes, jewelled ornaments, seven copper Gaṇapati images (two dancing), and lamps kept burning on ghee from the shepherds' cows and ewes (SII vol. II Nos. 84–94; Balasubrahmanyam 1975, pp. 16–27).
- Second source: The temple still stands; the second source describes its structure from the monument.
- Conjecture: Colours, paint and plaster, the amount of carving, the people, the lamps and flowers, the time of day. The tower is painted more heavily carved than the original. The dancing Gaṇapati carried on a platform is inferred from the seven Gaṇapati images (two dancing) in the inscriptions; the procession is not described. Dress is conjecture: no dress source was read. After review the first paintings were repainted to remove forms that look later than 1010 CE (stitched blouses and draped saris, turbans, a modern dance costume, machine-made tiles, tall gateway towers, woolly sheep); the current wrapped garments and chest bands follow Chola sculpture as the painter remembers it, not a cited source.

## gupta: Setting · Pāṭaliputra under Candragupta II, c. 405–411 CE

### The street: the yearly procession of image cars [street]
- Attested: Every year on the 8th of the 2nd month, a four-wheeled car bearing a five-storeyed bamboo structure, a little over 20 cubits high and tope-shaped, wrapped in white cloth painted in colours, with devas in gold, silver and lapis lazuli, streamers and canopies, and a seated Buddha with a standing Bodhisattva in niches on its four sides; about twenty different cars; singers, musicians, flowers and incense; Brahmans invite the Buddhas into the city; lamps burn all night (Faxian, ch. XXVII, tr. Legge 1886, p. 79).
- Second source: None: no second source read.
- Conjecture: The bullock team, the street architecture, the exact tier count, dress, the colours painted on the cloth.

### The monastery beside Aśoka's tope [temple]
- Attested: By the side of the tope of Aśoka a very grand and beautiful Mahāyāna monastery, and a Hīnayāna one, with 600–700 monks together; the great tope more than 3 li south of the city with a Buddha footprint and a vihāra before it; the palace halls with carved and inlaid stone, standing 'now as of old' (Faxian, ch. XXVII, pp. 77–80).
- Second source: None: no second source read.
- Conjecture: The tope's size and dome, monastery plans and materials, the lions, the vihāra's form.

### The house of charity and medicine [charity]
- Attested: Heads of Vaiśya families set up in the cities houses for charity and medicine; the poor, orphans, widowers, childless men, the maimed, cripples and the diseased go there and are given every help; doctors examine them and give food and medicine; they leave when better (Faxian, ch. XXVII, p. 79).
- Second source: None: no second source read.
- Conjecture: The building's form, furnishing, dress and number of people.

## hoysala: Setting · Viṣṇuvardhana's Velāpura (Belūr), 1117 CE

### The temple of Vijaya Nārāyaṇa: a lofty pile of pillars and lions [temple]
- Attested: The grant describes the temple set up by Viṣṇuvardhana as a lofty pile adorned with all the signs of fortune, filled with pillars, with lions and elephants among its decorations, with large chambers and a deep, peaceful interior, and adorned with numerous gilded pinnacles "towering up in innumerable storeys" (B. L. Rice, Mysore Inscriptions, translated for Government (Bangalore, 1879), pp. 264–269, no. 145 (Belūr, Śaka 1039 = 1117 CE)). Rice notes that the similes are puns, so the passage is poetry, not a plan.
- Other sources: None: no source read for the temple's form or its surviving fabric.
- Conjecture: The shape, plan, materials and carving. The "innumerable storeys" are poetic; the painting shows a stepped tower the text does not specify. The surviving Channakeśava temple at Belūr is not described in any source read here.

### The daily service: lamps, garlands, prayers and dancers [service]
- Attested: The king endowed the daily service, divine decoration and ceremonies of the three watches of the gods Vijaya Nārāyaṇa, Channa Keśava and Lakṣmī Nārāyaṇa, and the support of Vaiṣṇavas, Brahmans, learned men to write the prayers and songs, garland-makers, dancing girls for illumination, and all kinds of temple servants. His chief queen Śāntala Devī is called a crowning ornament of finished dancers (B. L. Rice, Mysore Inscriptions, translated for Government (Bangalore, 1879), pp. 264–269, no. 145 (Belūr, Śaka 1039 = 1117 CE)).
- Other sources: None.
- Conjecture: The hall, its pillars and hanging lamps, the lit images, the dance movements and costume, the queen's dress and the sacred fire. The draped garments on some women may be later than 1117.

### Velāpura: ramparts, moat and a king's palace (later witness) [city]
- Attested: Viṣṇuvardhana is said to be ruling "from his palace in the great city of Velapura" (B. L. Rice, Mysore Inscriptions, translated for Government (Bangalore, 1879), pp. 264–269, no. 145 (Belūr, Śaka 1039 = 1117 CE)).
- Later witness: A later Belūr grant of Vīra Ballāḷa's time praises a ruler whose "doors [are] securely fastened, his lodges filled with guards and archers, his ramparts high, surrounded with a moat — the lake named Vasudeva tīrtha" (same volume, pp. 266–267). It is not from Viṣṇuvardhana's day.
- Conjecture: The town's plan, gateway, wall and moat forms, the palace, houses and roofs. The red banners at the gate are the model's invention. The later grant may describe the temple precinct rather than a whole town.

## kamarupa: Setting · Bhāskaravarman Kumāra's Kāmarūpa, c. 643 CE

### A town of Kāmarūpa: water channels, jackfruit and coconut [city]
- Attested: The capital was about 30 li round; the land low but rich and regularly cultivated; jackfruit and coconut trees numerous and valued; water led from the river or banked-up lakes flowed round the towns; the climate soft and temperate; the people small in stature, dark yellow in complexion, honest, and earnest in study. They worshipped the Devas, with about 100 Deva temples, and had built no Buddhist monastery (Xuanzang, Si-yu-ki, Book X, tr. S. Beal, 1884, vol. 2 pp. 195–198).
- Other sources: None: no second source read for the town's buildings.
- Conjecture: The buildings, their materials and stilts, the footbridges, boat and buffalo, dress, and the rice fields and hills. The Deva temples are attested but their form is not described; the brick shrine with its tiered roof is the model's invention.

### King Kumāra receives the Chinese pilgrim [court]
- Attested: Bhāskaravarman, titled Kumāra, was a Brahman of the old line of Nārāyaṇa-deva, fond of learning, and honoured learned śramaṇas though no Buddhist himself; he invited Xuanzang three times, received him, said he had long loved men of conspicuous learning, and spoke of songs sung in India about the king of China (Xuanzang, Si-yu-ki, Book X, tr. S. Beal, 1884, vol. 2 pp. 195–198).
- Other sources: None: no second source read for the hall or the king's dress.
- Conjecture: The hall, its carving and furnishings, dress, crown and faces; the text records the exchange, not the setting. The small tiered tower seen in the distance is not supported by the text, and the manuscript bundle is not described.

## karkota: Setting · Lalitāditya's foundations in Kashmir, c. 725–760 CE

### The Mārtāṇḍa temple on the plateau of Mattan [temple]
- Attested: Kalhaṇa describes the Mārtāṇḍa shrine 'with its massive walls of stone within a lofty enclosure' (Rājataraṅgiṇī IV.192, tr. Stein 1900, vol. 1 pp. 141–42). Stein's note records the surviving ruins: a lofty central edifice, a small detached shrine on either side of the entrance, and a quadrangular courtyard of imposing dimensions surrounded by colonnades, on the arid plateau (udar) of Mattan.
- Later witness: Kalhaṇa wrote c. 1148, about four centuries after Lalitāditya, from tradition. The ruins are the check on his account.
- Conjecture: Roof form and ornament, the colonnade's design, the lamps, colours, season, the people. The temple is shown intact; today it is a ruin.

### Parihāsapura: pillar, shrines and the great Buddha [city]
- Attested: Lalitāditya built the town Parihāsapura, with a silver Parihāsakeśava, a golden Muktākeśava, a golden-armoured Mahāvarāha, a silver Govardhanadhara, a stone pillar 54 hands high topped by a Garuḍa, and the Rājavihāra with a large quadrangle, a large caitya and a large Jina image. A 'Great Buddha' of copper reaches up to the sky (Rājataraṅgiṇī IV.194–203, tr. Stein 1900). Only Kalhaṇa describes it; no remains are described in the text read.
- Later witness: Kalhaṇa, c. 1148, a later witness. No contemporary description of the town was read.
- Conjecture: All architecture and layout, the scale of the Buddha (Kalhaṇa's phrase is poetic), and its material, which is painted stone-coloured here although the text says copper. Dress, colours and the valley view are also conjecture. No street scene is made, as the source gives none.

## kushan: Setting · Kanishka's stūpa at Puruṣapura (Peshawar)

### Kanishka's great stūpa at Peshawar [temple]
- Attested: A stone stūpa of five storeys on a vast base (circumference 1½ li) with 25 gilded copper rings on a staff at the top, and a small stūpa at the south-east of the base; Faxian calls it the finest tope in India, 'more than four hundred cubits high, adorned with layers of all the precious substances' (Faxian, ch. XII, p. 34; Xuanzang, Book II, vol. 1 pp. 99–101).
- Later witnesses: Both pilgrims wrote 3–5 centuries after Kanishka and tell the legend of its building.
- Conjecture: The carved niches and Buddhas, the dome, the surrounding buildings and dress; the heights are legendary and scaled down. Both pilgrims saw the monument 3–5 centuries after Kanishka; no source describes a Kushan city or street, so only this scene is given.

## maitraka: Setting · Valabhī under the king Xuanzang met, c. 640 CE

### Valabhī, a merchant city of stored wealth [city]
- Attested: The capital was about 30 li round; the population very dense and the establishments rich; some hundred families possessed a hundred lakhs; the rare and valuable products of distant regions were stored there in great quantities; there were about a hundred monasteries with 6,000 monks and several hundred Deva temples (Xuanzang, Si-yu-ki, Book XI, tr. S. Beal, 1884, vol. 2 pp. 266–268).
- Second source: Valabhī stood at the head of Bhavnagar Creek and shared in Indian Ocean trade; Maitraka coins were struck in silver and copper, the copper ones with a trident and often a war-axe; the university drew comparison with Nālandā (I-tsing) (K. Dalal, "The Maitrakas", Live History India, 27 June 2021, citing H. P. Ray, Ars Orientalis 34, 2004).
- Conjecture: The street, houses, balconies, shop fronts, goods, dress and the camels. The monasteries and temples are attested but not shown, because the text puts the great monastery outside the city and gives no building form. A first draft placed a stūpa here; it was removed as not in the text.

### The king's seven-day charity to the monks [assembly]
- Attested: The king, a Kshatriya recently and sincerely devoted to the Buddhist faith, summoned a great assembly every year and for seven days gave away gems, exquisite food, the three garments and medicines to the monks, with costly articles of the seven precious substances; he afterwards redeemed them at twice their price. He honoured learned monks from distant lands (Xuanzang, Si-yu-ki, Book XI, tr. S. Beal, 1884, vol. 2 pp. 266–268). Beal's note records that Bühler and Cunningham identify the king with Dhruvabhaṭa, Burgess with Dhruvasena II.
- Second source: Dhruvasena II (627–641 CE) made gifts to both Buddhist and Brahmanical institutions (K. Dalal, "The Maitrakas", Live History India, 27 June 2021).
- Conjecture: The place, pavilion, courtyard, dress, crown, crowd and the look of the gifts: the text names none of them. The 'redeeming at twice the price' is not shown. A first draft showed a large stūpa, a lion column and banners; they were removed as not in the text.

## maurya: Setting · Pāṭaliputra under Candragupta, c. 321–297 BCE

### The city: Pāṭaliputra at the river confluence, c. 300 BCE [city]
- Attested: A long narrow city, about 80 stades by 15, at the meeting of two rivers, behind a timber palisade pierced for archers, with a ditch for defence and drainage (Megasthenes, in Strabo 15.1.35–36).
- Second source: Wide ditches with lotus and crocodiles, square towers, an arched gateway (Arthaśāstra 2.3, a prescription attributed to Kauṭilya; its date is debated).
- Conjecture: Roof forms, the palace hall, boats, vegetation, the light.

### The street: a royal road in the morning market [street]
- Attested: City commissioners supervised the markets, in boards of five (Megasthenes, in Strabo 15.1.50–51).
- Second source: Royal roads four daṇḍas (about 24 ft) wide; sellers of scents, garlands and grain on the east side; a well for every ten houses (Arthaśāstra 2.4).
- Conjecture: House fronts, dress, carts, the individual figures.

### The temple: a grand Śiva shrine, entrance facing north-east [temple]
- Attested: Temples (hiera) were under the commissioners' care (Megasthenes, in Strabo 15.1.51). No eyewitness describes one.
- Second source: At the centre of the city, the apartments of the gods, Śiva among them (Arthaśāstra 2.4).
- Conjecture: Everything visible: the brick plinth, timber hall, tiered tiled roof, the figure in the sanctum, its size, and its north-east facing.

## mysore: Setting · Seringapatam under Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan, as seen in 1800

### The city: Seringapatam, the island fort on the Cauvery [city]
- Attested: An island about 3 miles by 1 in the Cauvery, a large rapid river with a wide rocky bed; the fort at its upper end, long straight walls and square bastions, cavaliers heaped one above another, ditches cut through the granite, a high steep glacis, an inner rampart and wide inner ditch; a bridge of square granite pillars with long stones laid across them and an aqueduct beside the road; streets narrow and confused, houses mostly mean; the Śrī Raṅga temple of great antiquity (Francis Buchanan, A Journey from Madras through the Countries of Mysore, Canara and Malabar, 1807, vol. 1 pp. 58–66).
- Later witness: Buchanan saw the town in May 1800, a year after Tipu's fall and the siege, and describes it as he found it.
- Conjecture: The regularity of the walls (Buchanan calls the work 'unsightly'), the houses, the gateway tower's form, boats, dress.

### The Lal Bagh palace and Hyder's mausoleum [temple]
- Attested: At the lower end of the island, outside the walls, the Laul Bagh palace 'though built of mud, possesses a considerable degree of elegance' and is 'the handsomest native building' he had seen; the garden laid out with canals from the river; near it the mausoleum of Hyder, where Tipu also lies, ornamented with columns of black hornblende that takes a splendid polish; the tombs covered with rich cloths, mullahs praying and musicians playing the nobat; nearby the Dariya Daulat Bagh with painted walls (Buchanan, vol. 1 pp. 73–74).
- Later witness: Buchanan saw these buildings in 1800 and describes them as he found them.
- Conjecture: The palace reads as carved white stone here though Buchanan says mud; the dome, cypresses, planting layout and dress are conjecture.

## pallava: Setting · Kāñcīpuram and Māmallapuram under Narasimhavarman I, c. 640–650 CE

### Kāñcīpuram, the capital, as a Chinese pilgrim saw it c. 640 CE [city]
- Attested: The capital, Kāñcīpura, was about 30 li round; the soil fertile and regularly cultivated, with much grain and many flowers and fruits; hot climate; the people courageous, honest and fond of learning; some hundred monasteries with 10,000 monks of the Sthavira school of the Great Vehicle; some eighty temples of the gods; many Nirgranthas (naked Jain ascetics); stūpas built by Aśoka at places the Buddha had visited (Hiuen Tsiang, Si-yu-ki, Book X, tr. S. Beal, 1884, vol. 2 pp. 228–229).
- Other sources: None: no second source read for the city's buildings.
- Conjecture: All architecture (brick, timber and stucco, little of it surviving), layout, dress, and the mix of Buddhist and other buildings. The Nirgranthas are attested but not shown. The first painting had tall tapering temple towers, which belong to later centuries; this one shows only low buildings. No street, wall or palace is described in the text.

### Māmallapuram: a granite outcrop carved into a temple [temple]
- Attested: Seventeen inscriptions on the first, second and third storeys of the Dharmarāja Ratha record birudas (titles) of a Pallava king Narasimha, and a later king, Atyantakāma, took the ratha over and named it Atyantakāma-Pallaveśvara-gṛha; other temples at the site are called after the same king (E. Hultzsch, South-Indian Inscriptions vol. I, 1890, Nos. 1–23).
- Surviving monument: The monuments survive: the Dharmarāja Ratha is a monolithic, three-storeyed temple cut from a granite outcrop (from the surviving monument; not read in a source here).
- Conjecture: The carvers, scaffolds, tools and surrounding site; the sea, boat and palms. The painted tower looks built of blocks rather than cut from the outcrop, shows four tiers where the ratha has three storeys, and shows no visible inscription. The Shore Temple is later (under Rājasiṃha, c. 700) and is not shown.

## pushyabhuti: Setting · Kanyakubja (Kanauj) under Harṣa, c. 643 CE

### The city: Kanyakubja on the Ganges, c. 643 [city]
- Attested: Capital on the west, bordering the Ganges, about 20 li long and 4–5 li broad; a dry ditch round it and strong lofty towers facing one another; flowers, woods, lakes and ponds shining like mirrors on every side; rich houses and valuable merchandise; about 100 monasteries and 200 Deva temples (Xuanzang, Records, Book V, tr. Beal 1884, vol. 1 p. 206).
- Other sources: Beal's note, after Cunningham: the surviving citadel site is triangular with a ditch or dry channel on each side (vol. 1 p. 206 n.).
- Conjecture: Wall material and crenellation, tower and house forms, boats and sails, the domed building and other temples, dress.

### The street: Harṣa's procession with the golden Buddha [street]
- Attested: A golden Buddha about three feet high on a gorgeously caparisoned elephant; on its left Harṣa dressed as Śakra holding a precious canopy, on its right Kumāra-rāja as Brahmā with a white chāmara; 500 armoured elephants as escort to each; 100 elephants carrying drummers before and behind; highly decorated pavilions and stationed musicians along the way; pearls and gold and silver flowers scattered (Xuanzang, Book V, vol. 1 pp. 218–19).
- Second source: None: no second source read.
- Conjecture: The street buildings (the tall spires and domes in the background are later styles), dress cuts, elephant trappings, the crowd, the kings riding on the elephant instead of walking beside it.

### The monastery and tower at Kanyakubja [temple]
- Attested: A great sanghārāma on the west bank of the Ganges; to its east a 'precious tower' about 100 feet high with a golden statue of Buddha as tall as the king in the middle; a precious altar for washing the image on its south; a pavilion over the monastery gate; the assembly held among flowers (Xuanzang, Book V, vol. 1 pp. 218–19).
- Second source: None: no second source read.
- Conjecture: The tower's form (a carved brick spire here), the statue standing in an outside niche (the text says only 'in the middle'), the monastery plan, colours.

## rashtrakuta: Setting · The Balhara's kingdom in Amoghavarṣa I's time, c. 851 CE

### A city of the Balhara, with its Arab merchants [city]
- Attested: Sulaymān says the Balhara favours the Arabs more than any other Indian king, and that his subjects follow his example; he has immense wealth, and Tatariya dirhams are the coin of his country (Sulaymān the merchant, 851 CE, in Early Arab Geographers, in H. M. Elliot, History of India as told by its own historians, vol. 1, ed. J. Dowson, 1867). Iṣṭakhrī and Ibn Ḥawqal add that Muslims live in the cities of his realm, that Jāmiʿ mosques stand there, and that Muslims govern those cities for him (same volume). Al-Masʿūdī (943–44 CE) calls the capital, Mankir, "the great centre of India"; Elliot says its site is uncertain.
- Other sources: None: no second source read for any Rāṣṭrakūṭa city.
- Conjecture: The city, its layout, houses, dress and goods. Which city this is: Mankir cannot be placed, so this is a generic town. The mosque is not readable as one; the painting shows a pillared hall by a tank, and the text gives no mosque design. The camels are a guess.

### The Balhara's court: envoys, troops and elephants [court]
- Attested: Sulaymān calls the Balhara the most eminent prince of India, to whom every prince pays homage; his envoys to other princes are received with profound respect; he pays his troops regular wages, as is the practice among the Arabs, and has many horses and elephants and immense wealth (Sulaymān the merchant, 851 CE, in Early Arab Geographers, in H. M. Elliot, History of India as told by its own historians, vol. 1, ed. J. Dowson, 1867).
- Other sources: None: no second source read for the court or palace.
- Conjecture: The hall, throne, umbrella, dress, gifts and faces; no source describes the court. The scene shows foreign envoys, which the text does not state. The towers in the background are tall and tiered, which is not established for this date.

## vijayanagara: Setting · Vijayanagara under Kṛṣṇadevarāya, c. 1509–29

### The city: Vijayanagara inside its ring of hills, c. 1520 [city]
- Attested: The city lies inside rings of hills of piled white boulders; a stone wall with a water-filled moat where it crosses low ground and a row of upright pointed stones before it; a gate with a tower on each side; fields, gardens and two lakes inside the first wall; a palace enclosure larger than the castle of Lisbon; a great river on the north with round basket-boats (Domingos Paes, c. 1520–22, in Sewell, A Forgotten Empire, pp. 243, 253–9).
- Other sources: Abdur Razzaq (1443, a century earlier) counts seven walls and notes the same upright stones and the fields and gardens between the walls. Sewell reads his seven as successive defences on the approach (Sewell pp. 88–90).
- Conjecture: Roof colours, the layout of the streets, the boats' shapes, the light, the number and look of the towers.

### The street: the bazaar at the evening fair [street]
- Attested: A broad street of rich houses where merchants sell rubies, diamonds, pearls and cloth; an evening fair of horses, citrons, limes and oranges; a temple in every street; a car dragged through once a year; laden oxen and elephants crowding the streets; sacred bulls loose; men of every nation; most people barefoot (Paes, pp. 238, 253–7).
- Other sources: Abdur Razzaq (1443): long, broad bazaars with arcades and galleries, roses on sale everywhere, jewellers selling pearls and rubies openly (Sewell pp. 90–91).
- Conjecture: Facade forms, the arch of the distant gate, individual figures, colours of cloth and goods.

### The temple: the east gate of the great Hampi temple at dusk [temple]
- Attested: Paes's great pagoda, held in most veneration (his 'Adperadianar', which Sewell identifies as the Virūpākṣa temple): a street of arcaded pilgrims' houses before its east gate; a lofty gate tower covered in rows of men, women and hunting scenes, the figures shrinking toward the top; a second smaller gate and a pillared court; four columns before the temple, two gilded and two of copper; the gate and shrine clad in gilded copper with gilt tiger-like animals on the roof; 2,500–3,000 oil lamps lit nightly (Paes, pp. 260–1).
- Other sources: Sewell, citing the temple inscription, says Kṛṣṇadevarāya built a gopura and an assembly hall there at his coronation festival in 1509–10 and repaired another gopura (Sewell pp. 120, 161). The inscription itself was not read.
- Conjecture: The tower's proportions and bright colours, the sculpture style, the dress, the dusk light and the composition.
