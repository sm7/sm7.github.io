Royal emblems of India, with their evidence

Lāñchhana Registerराजलाञ्छन

Indian kingdoms marked their authority with an emblem: a boar on a seal ring, a tiger on a copper plate, a lion on a coin, an ape at the top of a war banner. This register collects those emblems and sets each one beside the evidence for it, saying plainly how strong that evidence is. Where the evidence allows, it also reconstructs how the emblem would have looked, as a painting made only from what the sources describe.

लाञ्छन lāñchhana: a mark or sign; in inscriptions, the emblem a dynasty set on its seal. ध्वज dhvaja: the banner and the emblem that crowned it.

Why this register exists

The emblems are well known. The evidence for them is not.

The evidence for India's royal emblems is scattered across epigraphy journals, museum catalogues, coin auctions and the verses of the epics. What circulates online mixes it with much later inventions: flags drawn in the last century, coats of arms made for British durbars, emblems assigned to dynasties that never used them.

This register puts each emblem next to its source. It quotes the verse, names the seal or coin, cites every sentence, and marks the difference between a seal that survives and a claim made later. Each emblem is also painted as a medallion in one consistent manner, after the palette of early Indian murals, so that emblems two thousand years apart can be compared side by side.

The paintings are reconstructions. The text is the evidence. Each entry's prompt is published as the record of how its painting was made, and where the two could be confused, the entry says so.

The boar, from Kurukṣetra to Vijayanagara

Jayadratha rides to war under a silver boar in the Mahābhārata. Centuries later the boar, now Viṣṇu's Varāha, is the seal of the Chalukyas, the coin of Mihira Bhoja, a disputed emblem of the Kākatīyas and the emblem of Vijayanagara, whose gold coin was simply called a varāha. How an entry is made

Source first, painting second

  1. Find the evidenceThe verse in the critical edition, the seal on a copper-plate grant, the coin type in a catalogue, or the earliest description of a flag.
  2. Check every sentenceEvery sentence carries its own citation, and an entry whose evidence is still unconfirmed says so.
  3. Reconstruct from the sourceAn image model paints the emblem with only the details the source gives. The prompt is published with each entry.
  4. Review and correctEach painting is compared with its source and redone when it adds or loses something. Corrections from readers are welcome.

Ground rules

What the register will not do

A register like this is only useful if a reader can trust what is left out as much as what is put in.

  • No invented emblems. A dynasty with no attested emblem is listed under "Left out, and why" rather than given one.
  • No silent upgrades. A coin type is labelled a coin, not a royal emblem; a later tradition is labelled tradition.
  • Dates on flags. A flag known only from the princely era, or a coat of arms made under the British, says so.
  • Edition stated. Epic verses follow the critical edition; passages found only in the vulgate are marked.
  • Reconstructions are not photographs. Where a photograph of the original seal or coin is available under an open licence, it is shown beside the painting.
