Royal insignia of Indian polities · c. 322 BCE – 1040 CE

Lāñchhana Registerराजलाञ्छन

The emblem a dynasty struck onto the seal ring of its copper-plate land grants was its lāñchhana, its official mark. Each emblem is painted by JavaScript in this page in the manner of early Indian mural and manuscript painting: flat mineral pigments, edge shading, brush contour and lotus-petal border. Where the dynasty's own region has surviving paintings, that tradition sets the palette; where none survive, the caption says so. Where no seal emblem survives, the dynasty's standard coin device is shown and labelled as a coin.

How to read the evidence tag

SealEmblem on the royal seal of copper-plate or clay-seal grants. This is the formal lāñchhana and the strongest evidence.
CoinRecurring coin device. It shows what the dynasty put on its money, not a declared insignia.
MonumentNo dynastic seal exists. The monument has become the dynasty's emblem by convention.

The paintings follow the attested design: its figure, posture and attributes. The style is an interpretation, not a copy of any one seal. The Tamil dynasties show their emblem word in Tamil. The rest show the Sanskrit term used in the grants.

Left out, and why

  • No verified emblem. Shunga, Vakataka, Later Guptas of Magadha, Karkota and Utpala of Kashmir, Shailodbhava, Bhaumakara, Somavamshi, early Kalachuri of Mahishmati, Chaulukya of Gujarat, Chandela, Chahamana, Kalabhra. I found no sources that describe a fixed seal emblem for these dynasties, so I drew none. Some are reported elsewhere (for example Gajalakshmi for the Somavamshis), but I could not confirm them.
  • After 1040 CE. The Sena (Sadāśiva), Gahadavala (Garuda and conch), Hoysala (Sala fighting a tiger), Kakatiya and Vijayanagara (varāha) emblems date from after 1040 CE.
  • Seal emblem uncertain. The Gurjara-Pratihāra seals are described as showing the goddess Bhagavatī, but I found no reliable account of her iconography. The card shows the Ādivarāha from Mihira Bhoja's coins instead.
